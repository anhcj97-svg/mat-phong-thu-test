### 4. Our source verification system
This is the most important part of the curriculum.
I propose a four-level source hierarchy.

Level A — Primary authority (Highest priority)
Official documentation, government agencies, platform policies, original research, technical standards and legislation.
Examples:
- TikTok Support for recommendation policies.
- Godot official documentation for engine behavior.
- US Copyright Office for US copyright matters.
- FTC for US advertising and endorsement rules.
- ACCC for Australian consumer protection.
- UK Advertising Standards Authority and CAP Code for UK advertising.

Level B — Recognized experts (Strong supporting evidence)
Established professional photographers, art directors, university researchers, industry associations and experienced practitioners with verifiable work.
Their advice is valuable, but distinguish professional opinion from formal rules.

Level C — Community experience (Use cautiously)
Reddit, Discord, YouTube comments, Fiverr seller experiences and creator discussions.
Useful for discovering problems, customer expectations and practical techniques. Not sufficient to establish platform policy or market-wide statistics.

Level D — AI-generated claims (Not evidence)
Answers from ChatGPT, Grok, Gemini, Claude and other models.
These can generate research questions and hypotheses. They cannot verify themselves.

The seven-question verification test
Before GPT recommends a new technique, policy or business decision, it must answer:
1. Who published this information?
2. Does the author have relevant qualifications or authority?
3. Is the source official, primary or secondary?
4. When was it published or last updated?
5. Does it apply to our country, platform and version?
6. Does the source actually support the claim being made?
7. Can an independent authoritative source confirm it?
If any important answer is missing, the information must be labelled uncertain.

How to resolve conflicting information
Suppose Grok says that changing a TikTok account's country improves US reach, while official TikTok documentation describes several recommendation signals.
We should not average the two answers.
Instead:
1. Retrieve the official documentation.
2. Identify exactly what it says.
3. Check whether the claim concerns account eligibility, location signals or recommendation ranking.
4. Separate confirmed facts from hypotheses.
5. Test any remaining practical hypothesis using our own account analytics.
TikTok's published documentation identifies user interactions, content information and user information as recommendation factors. It also says that interactions, including watch time, generally carry greater weight for many users.
That is a documented platform explanation, not a guarantee of how every individual video will perform.

Our evidence labels
Every research report should use these labels:
| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| VERIFIED | Directly supported by authoritative evidence |
| CORROBORATED | Independently supported by multiple credible sources |
| EXPERT OPINION | Professional judgment, not established fact |
| HYPOTHESIS | Plausible, but requires testing |
| UNKNOWN | Insufficient reliable information |
| OUTDATED | Previously supported, but no longer safe to rely on |

Important rule: Never convert UNKNOWN into VERIFIED simply because several AI models give the same answer. They may share the same incorrect information.
